Chapter 01

What is Game Theory?

Game Theory is the mathematical study of strategic interaction — situations where the outcome for each participant depends not just on their own choices, but on the choices of others.

It was formalized by mathematician John von Neumann and economist Oskar Morgenstern in 1944, and later revolutionized by John Nash, whose concept of equilibrium earned a Nobel Prize in 1994.

Core Definition

A game, in this context, is any situation with: (1) rational players, (2) available strategies for each player, and (3) payoffs that depend on the combination of chosen strategies.

Game theory is not about games like chess alone — it applies to:

🏛️

Politics

Arms races, coalition formation, voting behavior, international treaties.

📈

Economics

Price competition, auctions, bargaining, oligopoly markets.

🧬

Biology

Evolutionary strategies, animal behavior, cooperation in nature.

⚔️

Military

Battle strategies, nuclear deterrence, intelligence operations.

"Every man, wherever he goes, is encompassed by a cloud of comforting convictions — and game theory is the science that strips them away."

The key assumption: all players are rational — they aim to maximize their own payoff, and they know other players are rational too.

Chapter 02

The Building Blocks

Every game theory problem has three fundamental components:

1

Players

Decision-makers in the game. Can be individuals, firms, countries, or even nature itself. Denoted P₁, P₂, … Pₙ.

2

Strategies

A complete plan of action — what a player will do in every possible situation. A strategy set S = {s₁, s₂, …} contains all available options.

3

Payoffs (Utility)

The outcome — numerical reward or punishment — each player receives given the combination of strategies chosen. Written as u₁(s₁,s₂), u₂(s₁,s₂).

The Normal Form (Matrix)

The most common way to represent a game is as a payoff matrix. Rows = Player 1's strategies, Columns = Player 2's. Each cell shows (P1 payoff, P2 payoff).

Example: A Simple 2×2 Game
P2: LeftP2: Right
P1: Up(3, 3)(1, 4)
P1: Down(4, 1)(2, 2) ★
📌

How to read it: If P1 plays "Up" and P2 plays "Left", P1 gets 3 and P2 gets 3. The ★ marks a Nash Equilibrium — you'll learn exactly what that means in Chapter 4.

Rationality & Common Knowledge

Game theory assumes:

🧠

Rationality

Each player chooses the strategy that maximizes their own payoff, given their beliefs about what others will do.

🔄

Common Knowledge

"I know that you know that I know that you know…" — the game structure is known to everyone, infinitely.

Chapter 03

The Prisoner's Dilemma

The most famous game in all of game theory. Two suspects are arrested. They can't communicate. Each must choose: Cooperate (stay silent) or Defect (betray the other).

The Scenario

If both stay silent → each gets 1 year. If both betray → each gets 3 years. If one betrays and one stays silent → betrayer goes free, silent one gets 5 years.

Prisoner's Dilemma — Payoff Matrix (years in prison, lower = better)
B: Cooperate (Silent)B: Defect (Betray)
A: Cooperate(1, 1)(5, 0)
A: Defect(0, 5)(3, 3) ★

The tragedy: Defect is a dominant strategy for both — no matter what the other does, betraying is always "better" for you individually. Yet if both defect, both are worse off than if both had cooperated.

"Individual rationality leads to collective irrationality."

Chapter 04

Nash Equilibrium

Named after John Nash (depicted in A Beautiful Mind), a Nash Equilibrium is a set of strategies — one for each player — where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing their strategy, given what the others are doing.

Formal Definition

A strategy profile (s₁*, s₂*, …, sₙ*) is a Nash Equilibrium if for every player i:
uᵢ(sᵢ*, s₋ᵢ*) ≥ uᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ*) for all sᵢ ∈ Sᵢ

In plain English: given everyone else's strategy, you are playing your best response.

Think of it as a stable resting point — once reached, no one wants to deviate. It doesn't mean it's the best outcome (recall the Prisoner's Dilemma), just a stable one.

Finding Nash Equilibria: Best Response Method

For each player, find their best response to each of the other player's strategies. Where both players are simultaneously playing best responses → Nash Equilibrium.

Nash's Theorem

💡

Every finite game with mixed strategies has at least one Nash Equilibrium. This was Nash's landmark 1950 proof — a guarantee that stable outcomes always exist, even in complex games.

Classic Examples

Battle of the Sexes — Two Nash Equilibria
B: OperaB: Football
A: Opera(2, 1) ★(0, 0)
A: Football(0, 0)(1, 2) ★

Both players want to be together, but disagree on where. There are two pure strategy Nash Equilibria and one mixed strategy equilibrium.

Chapter 05

Types of Strategies

In game theory, a strategy is a complete contingent plan — specifying what to do in every possible situation, not just a single move.

PURE

Pure Strategy

Choose one specific action with certainty. "Always cooperate." No randomness involved.

MIXED

Mixed Strategy

Randomize over actions with given probabilities. "Cooperate 60% of the time." Opponent cannot predict you.

DOMINANT

Dominant Strategy

A strategy that is best regardless of what others do. Always pick this if it exists — it simplifies the game enormously.

Dominant Strategy Elimination

If a strategy is strictly dominated (another strategy always does better), rational players never choose it. We can eliminate it, then repeat for the simpler game — this is called Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies (IEDS).

Example

If "Middle" always gives P1 less than "Left" regardless of P2's choice → eliminate "Middle". Now solve the remaining 2×2 game. IEDS can sometimes solve the entire game down to a unique outcome.

Mixed Strategy Nash Equilibrium

When no pure strategy equilibrium exists (e.g., Rock-Paper-Scissors), players use mixed strategies. The key insight: in a mixed strategy equilibrium, each player must be indifferent between all strategies they mix over.

Rock-Paper-Scissors — Only Mixed Strategy NE
RockPaperScissors
Rock(0,0)(-1,1)(1,-1)
Paper(1,-1)(0,0)(-1,1)
Scissors(-1,1)(1,-1)(0,0)

The unique Nash Equilibrium: each player randomizes uniformly — ⅓ Rock, ⅓ Paper, ⅓ Scissors. Any deviation is exploitable.

Zero-Sum Games

In a zero-sum game, one player's gain is exactly another's loss. Total payoff always sums to zero. These are purely competitive.

⚖️

Von Neumann's Minimax Theorem: In every finite two-player zero-sum game, both players have an optimal mixed strategy. P1 maximizes their minimum gain; P2 minimizes P1's maximum gain. Both converge to the same value — the value of the game.

Sequential Games & Backward Induction

When players move in order (represented by a game tree), the solution method is backward induction: start at the last move, find what a rational player would do, then work backward to the first move.

Centipede Game Insight

Backward induction predicts players defect immediately in many sequential games — yet experiments show humans often cooperate far longer, revealing limits of pure rationality.

Chapter 07

Iterated Games & Cooperation

When the same game is played repeatedly, cooperation becomes rational. The shadow of the future changes everything — players can reward and punish each other across rounds.

🔁

Folk Theorem: In infinitely repeated games, virtually any outcome (including full cooperation) can be sustained as a Nash Equilibrium, as long as players are patient enough (discount factor δ is high).

Famous Strategies in Iterated PD

🤝

Tit-for-Tat

Start cooperating. Then copy your opponent's last move. Simple, forgiving once, never exploitable.

😇

Always Cooperate

Never defect. Easily exploited by defectors. Only works if everyone plays it.

😈

Always Defect

Always betray. Short-run gains, but loses against Tit-for-Tat in long runs.

💣

Grim Trigger

Cooperate until opponent defects once — then defect forever. Maximally punishing.

Chapter 08

Game Theory in the Real World

From your daily decisions to geopolitics, game theory is everywhere once you see it.

🏦

Auctions & Mechanism Design

How should a government auction spectrum licenses? Vickrey auctions (second-price) incentivize truthful bidding — the winner pays the second-highest bid, making honesty the dominant strategy. Google's AdWords uses this principle.

☢️

Nuclear Deterrence

The Cold War was a repeated game of Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD). The credible threat of total retaliation created a Nash Equilibrium of no first strike — neither side wants to deviate from "don't launch."

🚗

Traffic & Congestion

Braess's Paradox: adding a road can slow everyone down. Each driver individually optimizing their route creates a Nash Equilibrium that is collectively worse than if a central authority assigned routes.

🌿

Climate Negotiations

Each country prefers others to reduce emissions while it free-rides. This is a global N-player Prisoner's Dilemma. Without enforcement mechanisms, cooperation collapses — which is why binding treaties need sanctions.

💼

Labor Markets & Signaling

Why do employers demand degrees for jobs that don't need them? Spence's Signaling Model: education is a costly signal. Getting a degree proves you're productive (since it's cheaper for high types), even if it teaches nothing directly relevant.

"The theory of games is not merely a theory of games — it is a theory of strategic rationality itself."
